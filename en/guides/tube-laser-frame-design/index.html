Tube Laser vs Saw, Drill and Punch: Designing Self-Locating Box-Section Frames

Updated: 15 November 2026

A tube laser cuts the length, holes, slots and joint ends of a profile in one programmed setup. Sawing and drilling get there in several steps with marking out and re-clamping, and punching needs a die for every hole pattern. In box-section frames, tab-and-slot joints locate each member before welding, so frames weld faster and come out consistent.

Tube laser, saw and drill, or punching: which suits a box-section frame?

The short answer: for a profile part with several holes, slots or shaped ends that has to fit accurately into a frame, tube laser cutting is usually the better choice. Every feature is cut from one program without re-clamping, so all of them are positioned from the same datum, the end of the part. Sawing and drilling still do the job for straight cuts with few holes, and punching is built for a fixed hole pattern at very high volume.

How is a box-section frame made conventionally, and where do errors creep in?

The usual route: the tube is sawn to length, hole positions are measured and marked with a tape and square, centre-punched and drilled, and the ends are deburred with a grinder. Each part is clamped, unclamped and measured again several times.

That handling is exactly where errors get in: small tape and marking errors, clamp play at each step, and differences between operators. They add up and show at assembly as an out-of-square frame, mismatched hole spacing or open gaps, which means grinding to fit and filling gaps with weld.

To control this, shops usually build a welding jig, which has its own build cost and must be reworked whenever the design changes; on a small run, the jig can be a large share of the frame price.

What is a tab-and-slot frame, and why does it weld faster?

In a tab-and-slot joint, a tab is cut on the end of one frame member and a matching slot in the member it meets. When the tab drops into the slot, the member is located in length, width and angle: the parts find their own position, which is why these are called self-locating frames.

Instead of measuring and clamping each part, the welder slots the members together and tacks them. Fixturing is reduced, and because the cut geometry, not the operator, sets where each part sits, frames in a batch come out alike.

One point matters: tab and slot is a locating aid, not a replacement for the weld. The frame’s strength comes from the welds and the structural design; the tab only makes sure the weld goes in the right place.

Design rules for tab-and-slot box-section frames

Build these into the drawing from the start; HyperFab also reviews the drawing for manufacturability before cutting.

  • Fit clearance: leave a little clearance in tabs and slots so parts go together without hammering, but not so much that the slot stops locating.
  • Keep off the corner radii: box section has rounded corners, and a slot or hole that runs onto the radius changes shape. Keep feature edges on the flat part of the face.
  • Weld seam: welded tube and pipe carry a weld seam on the inside. If a tab or slot must not land on the seam, say so on the drawing.
  • Tab length and wall: match tab length to the wall of the mating member, and state whether the tab should finish flush or stand slightly proud for welding.
  • Wrong-way assembly: where you can, make features symmetric so orientation does not matter; otherwise make one tab or slot different so the part cannot go in backwards.
  • Part numbering: on multi-member frames, give every part a number on the drawing. Laser-marking the number on the part can be considered on request, depending on the section.

Coped (fish-mouth) and mitred joints in pipe and box section

When one pipe is welded onto the side of another, its end has to be shaped to the mating pipe. This is called a coped, fish-mouth or saddle cut. Instead of approximating it with a paper template and a grinder, a tube laser programs the cope from the real geometry of both pipes, so they sit together without gaps and weld more evenly. On thick-wall pipe, coping is confirmed after reviewing the drawing.

A mitre cut is an angled end on box section or profile for a frame corner. When two members meet on accurate mitres, the corner closes without a gap and squareness depends less on the welder’s skill. The achievable angle depends on the section. Sheet parts that go with the frame, such as gussets and base plates, can be ordered separately online through sheet metal laser cutting on hyperfab.ir.

When do punching or sawing still make sense?

Laser cutting is not always the cheapest route. If a part has only a few simple, repeated holes, the design is frozen and the volume is very high, tube and profile punching can be more economical once the die is made, because the die cost is spread across the whole run. HyperFab does punching too, and recommends the process that suits the geometry and quantity.

A saw is also fine for straight cuts on a few lengths with a hole or two. But the more holes, slots and shaped ends a part carries, and the tighter the frame has to be, the stronger the case for the laser.

From cut parts to a finished frame: welding, coating and what drives the price

After laser cutting, HyperFab can also carry out TIG and MIG welding of stainless steel and 6000-series aluminium frames to your approved design, followed by powder coating for batch orders; whoever cut the tabs and slots is then also responsible for the welding and final frame dimensions. For mild-steel frames, ask about welding when you request a quote; otherwise the parts are delivered cut and ready to assemble for your own welder.

A tube laser price cannot be given before the drawing is seen; it comes from a combination of these factors:

  • Section: square and rectangular tube, round pipe, angle, channel or special sections.
  • Material: mild steel, stainless steel or aluminium.
  • The number of cuts, holes, slots and shaped ends on each part.
  • Quantity: sample, small first run or batch order.
  • Whether you or HyperFab supply the material.
  • Follow-on work such as welding a stainless or aluminium frame, powder coating or anodizing.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Is a tab-and-slot frame strong enough, or is the tab only a locating aid?
It is a locating aid: tabs and slots hold the members in the right place before welding. The strength comes from the welds and the structural design, not from the tabs, so the frame should be welded and checked like any other welded structure.
Do I need a 3D file?
No, but it is the best input. A STEP or IGES model shows exactly how the tabs and slots relate. Without one, a 2D drawing, DXF or PDF is accepted, as long as the section and its dimensions are clearly stated.
Can you weld and paint the frame too?
For stainless steel and 6000-series aluminium frames, yes: after laser cutting, HyperFab carries out TIG and MIG welding to your drawing, followed by powder coating for batch orders. For mild-steel frames, ask about welding when you request a quote; cut parts can also be delivered ready for welding.
Which is cheaper for my quantity: laser or saw and drill?
It depends on how many holes, slots and shaped ends each part has, and on the quantity. Feature-rich parts usually favour the laser; a straight cut with a hole or two may favour the saw and drill. Send the drawing or call 09962233898 and HyperFab will recommend the right route.
