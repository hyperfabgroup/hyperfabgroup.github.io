How Sheet and Tube Punching Cost Is Determined

Updated: 15 November 2026

Sheet and tube punching is priced in two parts: a one-off cost to design and build the punching die, and a cost per stroke in production. The die cost depends on geometry, hole and slot count and required accuracy; the stroke cost on material, thickness, strokes per part and quantity. Larger runs shrink the die’s share of each part.

Short answer: what makes up the price of punching?

Punching makes holes, slots and shaped cut-outs in sheet and tube with a press stroke and a die. Because each part reaches its final shape in one or a few strokes, the price is not worked out from cut length the way laser cutting is. It has two separate parts instead: a die that is built once, and strokes that repeat throughout production.

HyperFab reviews the drawing, designs and builds a punching die for the material and thickness, punches a trial sample for your approval, and carries production and quality control through to delivery. The die cost and the cost per stroke are quoted separately after the drawing is reviewed. If HyperFab supplies the sheet or tube, material is added at current prices.

Die cost vs cost per stroke: what is the difference?

The two costs behave differently. The die is built once and its cost is spread across the whole run, while the stroke cost repeats with every part. On a short run the die dominates the price of each part; on a long run, strokes and material take over.

In practice, anything that simplifies the die pays off most on small quantities, and anything that cuts the number of strokes per part pays off most on large ones.

Punching or laser cutting: which is cheaper for your quantity?

Laser cutting needs no die, so it is cheaper for low quantities, prototypes and designs that are still changing. HyperFab’s metal laser cutting is an online service: upload a DXF file on hyperfab.ir and the price estimator quotes it instantly.

Punching is built for repeat runs with a fixed hole pattern. Once the die exists, each part is made faster and cheaper in one or a few strokes, and every part comes out the same. HyperFab offers both and recommends the right one for your quantity and geometry.

  • Low quantity, a prototype or a design that is not final yet: laser cutting.
  • Repeat runs with a fixed hole pattern: punching.
  • Holes and slots in tube, profile or polyethylene pipe: punching with a tube-specific die.
  • Somewhere in between: compare the online laser price with a punching quote for the same quantity.

How is tube and profile punching different from sheet?

Sheet lies flat and is fully supported on the die. Tube is round and hollow, and its wall deforms under the stroke. Tube and profile punching therefore usually needs a tube-specific die that holds the section in shape during the stroke, which is one reason a tube die is priced differently from a simple sheet die.

HyperFab punches holes and slots in metal tube and profile for structures, fittings, shelving, railings and furniture. On a tube drawing, give the hole positions relative to the tube end and to each other, and their orientation around the circumference, as well as the hole shape. All of these feed directly into the die design.

How is polyethylene (PE) pipe punched?

Punching polymer pipe is one of the harder jobs in this field. The pipe is round, the wall deforms under the stroke, and polyethylene behaves very differently from metal, so a die that works well on steel is not automatically right for PE.

HyperFab’s team has punched kidney-shaped slots in polyethylene pipe with a 125 mm diameter, 5 mm wall and 6-bar pressure rating. In another industrial project, the team was responsible for quality control of 8,000 punched tubes. Typical uses are drainage, filter, and water and air distribution pipes.

For a PE pipe quote, give the pipe diameter, wall thickness and pressure rating, the slot or hole shape, how it repeats along the length and around the circumference, and the quantity. A trial sample is punched before production so you can check the slots on the real pipe and approve them.

How to lower punching cost: design tips before the die is built

The die is the most expensive part of the project, and most savings are made before it is built. HyperFab reviews every design from the die’s point of view, and you can get ahead of that review with a few rules:

  • Keep holes a sensible distance from the edge; a hole too close to the edge distorts it under the stroke.
  • Size holes in proportion to the material thickness; a hole that is very small for the thickness puts heavy load on the punch.
  • Standardise hole sizes and shapes where you can; every new shape means another punch and a more complex die.
  • Put tight tolerances on functional dimensions only.
  • Freeze the design before the die is built; while you are still testing, make samples by laser cutting.
  • State the total quantity up front so the die cost is spread over the real number of parts.

What to send for a punching quote, and what drives the price

Send a drawing or a physical sample, the material and thickness, and an approximate quantity, or describe them by phone. For tube, add the diameter, wall thickness and hole positions along the length and around the circumference. HyperFab is based in Tehran and ships punched parts across Iran. The final price comes from the combination of the factors below, and no meaningful figure can be given before the drawing is reviewed.

  • Geometry, and the number and shape of holes and slots.
  • Die type: simple, progressive or tube-specific.
  • Required accuracy of dimensions and hole positions.
  • Material and thickness: mild steel, galvanised, stainless steel, aluminium or polymers such as polyethylene.
  • Strokes needed per part, and run quantity.
  • Whether HyperFab or the customer supplies the material.
  • Follow-on work such as powder coating the punched parts.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Who owns the punching die?
The die is designed and built for your part, and its cost is quoted once, separately from the cost per stroke. Agree ownership, storage and reuse of the die for later orders with HyperFab when you receive the quotation.
Is there a minimum quantity for punching?
Punching needs a die, so it suits repeat quantities; for low quantities, laser cutting is usually cheaper because there is no die cost. HyperFab recommends the right process for your quantity and geometry.
Can polyethylene pipe be punched cleanly?
Yes, provided the die is designed for how polyethylene behaves rather than for metal. HyperFab’s team has punched kidney-shaped slots in 125 mm PE pipe with a 5 mm wall and 6-bar rating, and a trial sample is punched for your approval before production.
Punching or laser cutting for a few hundred parts?
It depends on the geometry and whether the order repeats: with a fixed design and repeat orders, the die cost is spread over every run and punching can pay off. For a one-off batch or a design that may change, laser cutting is usually cheaper. Get an instant laser price on hyperfab.ir and compare it with a punching quote.
