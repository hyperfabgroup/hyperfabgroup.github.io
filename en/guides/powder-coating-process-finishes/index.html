Powder Coating: Process, Powder Types and Finish Choices

Updated: 15 November 2026

Powder coating is a dry powder that is electrostatically charged, sprayed onto a metal part and melted and cured in an oven into a hard, even layer. How well it performs depends on surface preparation, the powder chemistry (epoxy, polyester or hybrid) and a correct cure, while gloss and texture should be chosen to suit the part's use.

Powder types: epoxy, polyester or hybrid?

Before you pick a colour or texture, the most important decision is the resin the powder is based on, because it decides how the coating behaves outdoors and in sunlight.

The rule of thumb is simple: parts that see sunlight need polyester; where chemical resistance and adhesion indoors matter most, epoxy; and for most indoor parts where looks matter, a hybrid. For harsher conditions, such as coastal sites, the preparation and powder should be agreed together from the start.

Why is surface preparation the most important step?

Powder only bonds well to a clean, properly prepared surface. Most failures that show up months later as flaking or rust creeping under the coating trace back to poor preparation, not to the powder itself.

Welded parts need extra care: spatter, slag, sharp edges and open seams must be dealt with before coating, because the powder will not hide them. Castings and galvanized parts can also release gas during curing and need specific preparation. Depending on the material and its condition, preparation may include:

  • Degreasing to remove cutting oil, grease and handling marks.
  • Removing rust, mill scale or oxide, mechanically by abrasive blasting or chemically.
  • A conversion coating for better adhesion and corrosion resistance: phosphating on steel, and chromate (Alodine) or its alternatives on aluminium.
  • Thorough rinsing and drying, because trapped moisture ends up sealed under the powder.

How is powder applied and cured?

After preparation, parts are hung on hooks or racks in a spray booth. The electrostatic gun charges the powder particles and the grounded part attracts them, which is why powder also wraps around edges and onto the back of the part. Overspray is usually collected and reused.

The part then goes into the oven. The powder melts, flows out over the surface and then cures chemically into a hard, continuous film. The cure schedule is set by the powder manufacturer and has to account for the metal thickness, since heavy parts take longer to reach temperature.

Two design points follow from this. First, the part must survive the oven, so plastic and rubber parts, seals and bonded assemblies have to be removed beforehand. Second, the hanging point stays uncoated, so plan a hole or tab for it. Threads, precision bores and grounding points are usually masked with heat-resistant plugs or tape.

Gloss, matte, texture or pearl: choosing a finish and RAL colour

Specify colour by its standard RAL code rather than a description or photo, since screens and prints never show colour accurately. Pearl and metallic powders can vary slightly between powder batches; if parts will sit side by side, coat them in one order and approve a reference sample. Some metallic powders need a clear topcoat for outdoor use.

Once the powder type is settled, gloss and texture set the final look:

  • Gloss: makes colours look richer and is easy to clean, but shows surface unevenness and scratches more.
  • Satin and matte: a calmer, more industrial look that hides minor surface flaws better.
  • Texture and wrinkle: a rough surface that hides fingerprints and scratches; good for housings and high-touch parts.
  • Hammer: looks like hammered metal and hides imperfections; common on cabinets, tools and machinery.
  • Pearl and metallic: metallic or pearlescent particles give a lustrous sheen for cosmetic parts and premium products.

Common powder coating defects and how to prevent them

Most powder coating defects can be prevented, and the cause is usually preparation, part design or curing. Adhesion is typically checked with a cross-cut test per ISO 2409. The usual suspects:

  • Flaking and poor adhesion: usually inadequate degreasing or preparation.
  • Pinholes and blisters (outgassing): gas escaping from castings, galvanized steel or weld seams during curing; reduced by proper preparation and pre-heating.
  • Orange peel: a wavy surface caused by the wrong film build or curing conditions.
  • Thin coverage in inside corners: the Faraday cage effect keeps powder out of deep recesses; more open corners in the design help.
  • Craters: contamination with oil or silicone; solved by cleaning and keeping silicone sprays away from parts before coating.
  • Yellowing or fading: over-curing, or using epoxy powder outdoors.
  • Rust starting at edges: powder builds thin on sharp edges, so chamfer or round them before coating.

Powder coating vs liquid paint: what is the difference?

Powder coating builds a thicker, harder film than liquid paint in a single coat and stands up better to impact, scratches and corrosion. It contains no solvent, does not run, and overspray can be recovered. It is also more consistent across a batch, because film build and appearance repeat from part to part.

Liquid paint is still the better choice in a few cases: parts that cannot take oven heat, such as plastics and wood; structures larger than the oven or that must be painted on site; and local touch-ups. Repairing damaged powder coating is also harder, and is usually done with a matching liquid paint.

What affects the cost of powder coating?

Each order is quoted after the parts have been reviewed. The main factors are:

  • Part size and total surface area, and how many parts fit in the booth and oven at once.
  • Weight and metal thickness, which set how long the part takes to reach curing temperature.
  • Powder type (epoxy, polyester or hybrid) and special effects such as pearl, metallic or hammer.
  • Preparation required: simple degreasing, abrasive blasting, phosphating or chromate.
  • Masking of threads, holes and uncoated surfaces.
  • Uncommon colours and colour changes between orders.
  • Quantity: HyperFab's powder coating service is for production runs, and one-off parts are accepted only rarely.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Powder coating or liquid paint?
For batch metal parts that can take oven heat, powder coating is usually more durable and more consistent. Liquid paint is better suited to heat-sensitive parts, very large structures, on-site painting and touch-ups.
Is powder coating suitable for outdoor use?
Yes, with the right powder. Polyester powders are made for outdoor exposure and sunlight, while epoxy and hybrid powders chalk and fade in the sun. Good surface preparation matters just as much for outdoor durability.
Can aluminium be powder coated?
Yes. HyperFab powder-coats steel, iron, aluminium and other metals. On aluminium, a chromate (Alodine) base improves paint adhesion and corrosion resistance.
What limits the size of a part for powder coating?
The size of the oven and spray booth, the hanging capacity and the part's weight. Heavy, thick parts also take longer to reach curing temperature. For large parts and frames, give the dimensions and weight before ordering so feasibility can be checked.
