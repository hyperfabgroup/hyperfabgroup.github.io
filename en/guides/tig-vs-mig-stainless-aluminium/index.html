TIG vs MIG Welding for Stainless Steel and 6000-Series Aluminium

Updated: 15 November 2026

TIG welding uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode with filler added separately, giving clean, precise, spatter-free welds that suit thin sheet, medical equipment and cosmetic parts. MIG feeds wire continuously, so it is faster and cheaper on long structural seams. The right choice depends on material, thickness, required finish and the weld tests your project specifies.

What is the difference between TIG and MIG welding?

The short answer: TIG is slower but more controllable and leaves a clean, even bead; MIG is faster and cheaper on long and structural welds. Neither is better across the board. Material, thickness, joint type and any cosmetic or hygiene requirements decide which one fits.

A note on names, since Iranian suppliers use Persian trade terms: “argon welding” usually means TIG, while “CO2 welding” means continuous-wire welding, the MIG/MAG family. Strictly, MIG uses an inert shielding gas such as argon and is the wire process for aluminium; with an active gas such as CO2 on carbon steel, the correct name is MAG. So “CO2 welding” is really MAG, and aluminium is never welded under CO2. TIG also runs on argon, which is why both processes sometimes get called argon welding.

What is TIG welding and where is it used?

In TIG welding (Tungsten Inert Gas, standardised as GTAW), the arc burns between a non-consumable tungsten electrode and the workpiece while argon shields the pool from the air. Filler is added separately, so the welder controls heat and deposited metal independently and has fine command of bead shape and penetration.

The result is a narrow, clean, spatter-free weld, which is why TIG is the default for thin stainless sheet and tube, cosmetic parts, root passes, and medical and food equipment. Its weakness is speed: on long seams or thick sections labour hours and cost climb quickly.

What is MIG welding, and how does it relate to “CO2 welding”?

In MIG welding (Metal Inert Gas, standardised as GMAW), wire feeds continuously from a spool through the torch and serves as both electrode and filler. Deposition is high, so long seams fill faster and more consistently.

What separates MIG from market “CO2 welding” is the shielding gas: inert for MIG, active for MAG. Aluminium needs inert gas, and stainless is normally welded with argon-based mixtures. Pulsed MIG reduces spatter and heat input, but the bead rarely looks as even as TIG. MIG earns its place on chassis, frames, machine bodies and large structures, where speed and cost matter most.

Welding stainless steel: heat tint, pickling and passivation

Stainless steel resists corrosion thanks to an extremely thin chromium-oxide film. Welding heat leaves straw-to-blue colours beside the seam, called heat tint. The darker the tint, the more chromium-depleted the surface beneath it and the sooner it rusts. Controlling heat input helps, and on tube and vessels the back of the joint is purged with argon (back purging).

After welding, heat tint is usually removed by pickling or mechanical means, and the surface is passivated so the protective film reforms evenly. The details depend on the grade, service environment and project specification, so they belong on the drawing or order. Tools and brushes reserved for stainless matter too, so no carbon-steel particles are left on the surface.

In medical, food and sanitary equipment, welds must be smooth, free of crevices and pores, and easy to clean so contamination has nowhere to collect; full-penetration TIG with dressed beads is the norm. HyperFab has built many units for the medical and marine industries.

Welding 6061 and 6063 aluminium: what to watch for

Aluminium is always covered by a thin, tough oxide skin that melts at a far higher temperature than the metal underneath. If it is not broken up, the joint does not fuse properly and oxide gets trapped in the weld. That is why aluminium is TIG welded on alternating current (AC): during the electrode-positive half-cycle the arc scours away the oxide (cleaning action), and during the other half more heat goes into the work for penetration. In MIG the wire runs electrode-positive, which gives the same cleaning action. Degreasing and brushing the joint just before welding also matter, because moisture and grease are the main causes of porosity.

The 6000-series alloys, such as 6061 and 6063, owe much of their strength to heat treatment. Welding heat partly undoes it in the heat-affected zone (HAZ), leaving that band softer than the parent metal. This happens with both TIG and MIG and must be allowed for in the structural design. These alloys are also prone to hot cracking without a suitable filler, so filler selection is part of the welding procedure.

Weld inspection: when to use VT, PT or PAUT

HyperFab carries out visual testing (VT), liquid penetrant testing (PT), phased-array ultrasonic testing (PAUT) and other tests on request. Which one you need depends on the defect you are worried about:

Agree acceptance criteria up front too. For fusion welds in steel, including stainless, quality levels are typically specified per ISO 5817, and for arc welds in aluminium per ISO 10042; NDT personnel qualification is commonly requested per ISO 9712. If your project calls for a particular standard or quality level, state it with the drawings.

  • VT (visual testing): the baseline for every weld; checks weld shape and size, surface cracks, undercut, spatter and heat tint.
  • PT (liquid penetrant): reveals surface-breaking cracks and discontinuities; the usual surface method for stainless steel and aluminium, which are generally non-magnetic.
  • PAUT (phased-array ultrasonics): finds internal defects such as lack of fusion, internal cracks and porosity, with a cross-sectional image of the weld; used on full-penetration and load-bearing structural joints.

What drives the cost of TIG and MIG welding?

Structural design is usually the client’s, and HyperFab fabricates to your approved drawings (on some projects HyperFab has also designed the mechanism and frame). The drawings are first checked for manufacturability, welding sequence and distortion control, and then quoted. The main cost drivers are:

  • Material: stainless steel, 6000-series aluminium or St37 steel (which HyperFab welds by TIG)
  • Weld length and type, and the process; TIG is slower, MIG faster
  • Structure size and fixturing; on large frames, such as a framed chassis mounted on wheels that runs on rails, flatness and alignment over long lengths drive the effort
  • Cosmetic and hygiene requirements: weld dressing, back purging and heat-tint removal
  • Requested tests: VT, PT, PAUT or others
  • Quantity and how repeatable the work is

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Which is stronger, TIG or MIG?
Neither is inherently stronger; joint strength comes from full fusion and penetration, the right filler, and freedom from defects such as porosity and lack of fusion. On 6000-series aluminium, HAZ softening limits strength whichever process is used.
Why does aluminium need AC TIG?
Aluminium’s oxide skin melts at a far higher temperature than the metal itself. On alternating current, the electrode-positive half-cycle breaks up that oxide and the other half delivers heat and penetration; without this cleaning action, oxide is trapped in the weld.
What does PT detect?
Liquid penetrant testing (PT) reveals cracks, pores and other discontinuities open to the surface. A dyed liquid seeps into the flaw and, once the surface is cleaned and developer applied, the indication shows. It cannot find fully internal defects; those need ultrasonic testing.
When is PAUT needed?
When internal defects such as lack of fusion, internal cracks or porosity matter for safety or function, typically in full-penetration and load-bearing structural welds.
