From Design to Production: Injection Mould Making Step by Step

Updated: 15 November 2026

An injection moulding project runs from part design for moulding, through mould design and steel selection, CNC machining, EDM and polishing, to trial shots, corrections and finally production. Most of the risk sits in the early stages, so every change made before steel is cut costs far less than one made afterwards.

What are the stages of injection mould making?

Mould making (mold making in US spelling) and injection moulding are two halves of one project. First a steel mould is built that carries the shape of the part; then an injection moulding machine fills it with molten polymer, shot after shot. Much of the final part quality is decided on the day the part design and mould design are frozen.

At HyperFab the route starts with a mouldability review of the design. The mould is then designed and built, sampling and correction continue until you approve the part, and production follows with quality control.

Designing a part for injection moulding: draft, wall thickness and ribs

A design that prints or machines well is not necessarily mouldable. The first rule is draft: walls parallel to the direction the mould opens need a slight taper so the part releases without scuffing or sticking. The right amount depends on the material, wall depth and surface texture, and is set during the design review.

The second rule is uniform wall thickness. Polymer shrinks as it cools, and thick sections cool last, which causes warping, sink marks and internal voids. Instead of thick walls, use ribs for stiffness and bosses for screw points. A rib should be thinner than the wall it supports so it does not leave a mark on the opposite face.

Undercuts, features that stop the part coming straight out of the mould, need slides or lifters and make the mould more expensive; a small design change can often remove them. Sharp internal corners are better rounded, which helps material flow and reduces stress concentration. Tolerances should be realistic for the chosen material and its shrinkage.

Mould design and choosing the mould steel

Mould design settles a few key questions: where the parting line goes, how many cavities the mould has, where and through what kind of runner (cold or hot runner) the material enters, how the cooling channels cool the part evenly, and where the ejector pins sit. Gate position directly affects the part’s appearance, weld lines and warpage.

Shrinkage is built into the cavity dimensions: the cavity is cut slightly larger than the finished part, by an amount that depends on the material. That is why the material must be fixed before the mould is built. Switching later, say from polyethylene to ABS, may mean modifying the mould.

Steel choice depends on volume, polymer and the finish required. Pre-hardened mould steels suit many medium-volume projects and are easier to machine. For very high volumes or abrasive materials such as glass-filled grades, a hardened tool steel lasts longer, and stainless mould steel is used for corrosive polymers or high-gloss finishes.

Building the mould: CNC machining, EDM and polishing

Most of the cavity and core material is removed by CNC milling. Where a cutter cannot reach, such as deep narrow ribs, sharp internal corners and fine detail, the shape is made by spark erosion, or electrical discharge machining (EDM): a graphite or copper electrode copies its form into the steel. Wire EDM is also used for precise holes and inserts.

After machining, the cavity surfaces are polished. The level of polish depends on the part: a hidden industrial part needs only a basic finish, while a cosmetic or transparent part needs a high gloss. If a textured surface is required, say so at the start, because texture affects the draft the part needs. Finally the slides, ejector pins and cooling circuit are assembled and the mould is ready for trials.

What are T0 and T1 trial shots, and what gets corrected?

The first test moulding with a new tool is usually called T0. Its purpose is to find problems, not to produce finished parts. The trial checks whether the cavity fills completely or gives short shots, whether flash appears on the parting line, and looks for sink marks, warpage, ejector marks and how easily the part releases. Samples are measured and compared with the drawing.

Some issues are solved by adjusting process settings such as pressure, speed and cooling time; others need steel to be modified. Toolmakers often cut critical dimensions steel-safe, leaving a little extra metal, because removing steel is easier than adding it. After corrections, the next trial (T1, and further rounds if needed) brings the part into line with the drawing.

At HyperFab, samples are moulded once the mould is built, and production starts only after you approve them.

How does the production run work?

Once a sample is approved, the process settings are locked and recorded so that every production run produces the same part. Raw material is prepared before moulding; some polymers, such as nylon and polycarbonate, absorb moisture and are dried first. During production, parts are checked at intervals for dimensions and appearance, and the mould itself needs care: cleaning, lubricating the slides and checking the cooling circuit.

HyperFab carries out production runs in polyethylene, polypropylene, ABS, nylon and engineering thermoplastics such as polycarbonate and acetal. Its team has taken four polyethylene injection moulds from build supervision to commissioning, and more than 80,000 parts have been produced with them. Orders for this service are arranged by phone.

What drives the cost of mould making and injection moulding?

An injection project is priced in two parts, quoted separately: a one-off mould cost and a cost per shot in production. The main drivers are:

  • Part size and complexity, and therefore mould size.
  • Number of cavities.
  • Undercuts and the slides or lifters they require.
  • Required surface finish, polish or texture.
  • Mould steel and runner type (cold or hot runner).
  • Number of trial rounds and design changes after the build has started.
  • Per shot: polymer type, grade and colour, part weight, cycle time and run quantity.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What drives mould cost?
The main factors are part size and complexity, the number of cavities, undercuts and slides, surface finish and the mould steel. HyperFab quotes the mould separately from the cost per shot after reviewing the design.
How many cavities do I need?
It depends on total volume, part size, the injection machine’s capacity and your budget. A single-cavity mould is cheaper to build but keeps the cost per part higher; a multi-cavity mould is the reverse. The right number is recommended during the design review, based on your expected volumes.
Who owns the mould?
By common practice, a mould the customer has paid for normally belongs to the customer. Even so, ownership, storage location, maintenance responsibility and the right to move the mould should be set out in writing in the agreement. Raise these points with HyperFab when you request a quote.
Can a 3D-printed prototype come first?
Yes, and it is a good idea. An FDM 3D print lets you check form, dimensions and assembly on a real part, and you can price it online at hyperfab.ir. Keep in mind that a printed part does not have the mechanical properties or surface of a moulded one, so it mainly confirms geometry.
