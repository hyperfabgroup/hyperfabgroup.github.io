MO40 vs CK45 Steel for Gauges and Turned Parts

Updated: 15 November 2026

MO40 (42CrMo4, 1.7225) is a chromium-molybdenum alloy steel; CK45 (C45E, 1.1191) is a plain medium-carbon steel. MO40 hardens deeper and more evenly and stays tougher, so it suits heavily used gauges and loaded parts. CK45 costs less and responds well to induction hardening, which makes it the economical choice for simple gauges and general turned parts.

MO40 vs CK45 at a glance

The short answer: MO40 is a chromium-molybdenum alloy steel, CK45 is a plain medium-carbon steel. Their carbon content is similar, so the surface hardness each reaches after quenching is not very different. What separates them is how deep and how evenly they harden, how tough they are after heat treatment, and what they cost.

If a part works under load, impact or constant wear, or has a thick section that must harden through to the core, MO40 is the safer pick. If the part is simple, lightly loaded, or only needs a hard working surface, CK45 does the job for less money. The table compares the two qualitatively.

PropertyMO40 (42CrMo4)CK45 (C45E)
Steel typeLow-alloy chromium-molybdenum steelPlain medium-carbon steel
Hardenability (depth of hardening)High; thicker sections harden through to the coreLow; thick sections mainly harden at the surface
Toughness after heat treatmentHighModerate
MachinabilityGood; harder on tools when bought quenched and temperedGood and predictable, especially when normalised
Typical usesLoaded and impact-loaded shafts and parts, heavily used gaugesGeneral shafts and pins, machine parts, simple gauges
Relative costHigherLower

What are the equivalents of MO40 and CK45, and how does their composition differ?

MO40 is the name the Iranian market uses for 42CrMo4, material number 1.7225. It is a medium-carbon steel with chromium and molybdenum added, and those two elements are what raise its hardenability and toughness. The closest American equivalent is AISI 4140.

CK45 is the old German DIN name for the steel now designated C45E, material number 1.1191. It is a plain medium-carbon steel with no significant alloying. The "k" in the old name refers to tighter control of impurities such as phosphorus and sulphur. Close equivalents are C45 and AISI 1045.

Equivalents are close, not identical. Element ranges differ slightly between standards, and bar sold under one name in the market is not always exactly that grade. For gauges and other critical parts, ask for the mill certificate or have the material checked by analysis.

Which one hardens better? MO40 vs CK45 heat treatment

It helps to separate hardness from hardenability. Hardness is how hard the surface gets after quenching, and it depends mostly on carbon content, which is similar in these two steels. Hardenability is how deep that hardness reaches below the surface, and this is where the chromium and molybdenum in MO40 make the difference.

MO40 hardens to a good depth even with a milder quench. That makes it well suited to through-hardening followed by tempering (quench and temper), after which it is both strong and tough. It is also widely sold already quenched and tempered. CK45 needs a more severe quench to harden, which raises the risk of distortion and cracking, and in thick sections it effectively hardens only in an outer layer.

That is why CK45 is the usual candidate for surface hardening: induction or flame hardening puts a hard skin on the working surface while the core stays tough. MO40 can be surface hardened or nitrided too, when a wear-resistant surface over a strong core is needed.

  • Through-hardening and tempering: the natural route for MO40, especially in thick sections.
  • Induction or flame hardening: the common route for CK45 shafts, pins and wear surfaces.
  • Nitriding: an option for MO40 when a hard surface is needed with minimal dimensional change.
  • Either way: the part is turned before heat treatment with enough stock left for final grinding.

Is MO40 or CK45 easier to machine?

In the normalised or annealed condition both steels machine well, and CK45 is usually a little easier and cheaper to turn. When MO40 is bought quenched and tempered it is stronger, tools wear faster and cutting has to slow down, which feeds straight into machine time and cost per part.

The order of operations matters as much as the grade. A part that will be hardened is typically rough-turned first, stress relieved if needed, then heat treated, and finally ground or finished on the precision surfaces. If that sequence is not stated on the drawing or in the enquiry, a part turned accurately in the soft state can distort in heat treatment and end up out of tolerance.

In HyperFab's batch CNC turning, each part's critical points are engineered and briefed to the machinist before production: fit surfaces, critical diameters, concentricity and the grinding allowance. That keeps the production route matched to the material and to what the part actually has to do.

Which steel is better for gauges, MO40 or CK45?

A gauge is the precision reference of a production line, and any error in it passes straight into the product. Three things drive the material choice: wear resistance on the measuring surfaces, dimensional stability over time, and the ability to survive knocks and drops on the shop floor.

Gauges are usually heat treated after turning, and their measuring surfaces are ground so they end up both hard and accurate. Because MO40 hardens deeper and more evenly and moves less in heat treatment, it is the safer choice for heavily used gauges, thick-section gauges, and gauges with thin lips or slender features. CK45 is an economical choice for simple, lightly used gauges, or for gauges where only the working surface is induction hardened.

For master gauges or gauges facing very severe wear, designers often move to tool steels instead. HyperFab has produced batch gauge parts in both MO40 and CK45, and those parts were approved by the customers. The final material call belongs to the gauge designer, and it can be discussed during the drawing review.

What drives the cost of an MO40 or CK45 turned part?

Material is where the price starts, but not where it ends. These factors carry the most weight:

  • Grade and bar diameter: MO40 costs more than CK45, and unusual diameters can be more expensive to source.
  • Supply condition: machining quenched-and-tempered MO40 takes more machine time and tooling.
  • Heat treatment and grinding: the hardening method (through, induction or nitriding) and how many surfaces need grinding.
  • Tolerances and surface finish: more fit surfaces and critical diameters mean more time and inspection. General tolerances are typically specified per ISO 2768 on the drawing.
  • Part complexity: the number of steps, grooves, threads and secondary operations.
  • Quantity: setup and first-article approval are spread over more parts.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is MO40's equivalent grade?
MO40 is 42CrMo4, material number 1.7225, and its closest American equivalent is AISI 4140. These equivalents are close rather than identical, so check the mill certificate for critical parts.
Which gets harder after heat treatment, MO40 or CK45?
Their surface hardness after quenching is similar, because their carbon content is similar. The difference is depth: MO40 hardens deeper and more evenly and is tougher after tempering, while CK45 in thick sections hardens mainly at the surface.
Can CK45 be induction hardened?
Yes, and it is one of CK45's most common uses. Induction hardening gives a shaft, pin or gauge a hard working surface while the core stays tough. Precision surfaces are usually ground after hardening.
Which lasts longer as a gauge, MO40 or CK45?
Under the same working conditions and with proper heat treatment, an MO40 gauge usually holds its dimensions better and resists wear and impact longer. For a lightly used gauge, hardened CK45 is often enough and costs less. The quality of the heat treatment and grinding matters as much as the grade.
