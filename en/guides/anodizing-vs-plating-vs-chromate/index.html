Anodizing vs Electroplating vs Chromate Conversion: Which Finish?

Updated: 15 November 2026

Anodizing grows a hard, insulating oxide out of the aluminium itself. Chromate conversion (Alodine) forms a very thin, conductive film on aluminium. Electroplating deposits a different metal, such as silver or gold, onto brass and other metals. Start with the base metal, then decide whether conductivity, wear resistance or appearance matters most for the part.

Anodizing vs plating vs chromate at a glance

These three finishes get mixed up because they all count as surface treatment, but they work in completely different ways. Anodizing and chromate conversion act on the aluminium itself and turn its surface into a new compound; electroplating deposits a layer of another metal on top of the part. That difference drives everything else: conductivity, appearance and even whether the part's dimensions change.

What is aluminium anodizing and what colours are available?

Anodizing (also spelled anodising) is an electrochemical process in which the aluminium part is made the anode of a circuit, so the thin natural oxide on its surface is thickened in a controlled way. The layer is not paint sitting on the metal: it grows out of the aluminium, which is why it does not flake. The result is a harder surface with better corrosion and wear resistance.

Anodizing is for aluminium, and the alloy affects the result: 6000-series extrusion alloys usually anodize evenly, while high-copper and casting alloys can come out duller and patchier.

A key advantage of anodizing is that it can be coloured. The fresh oxide is porous and absorbs dye before sealing; sealing then locks the colour inside the layer. The surface finish beforehand matters as much as the dye: a bead-blasted part comes out evenly matt, a polished one brighter. Common options:

  • Natural or clear: keeps the silver look of the aluminium.
  • Black: common on device housings, optical parts and tools.
  • Dyed colours such as gold, red or blue: for cosmetic and decorative parts.

What is chromate conversion (Alodine) and why does it stay conductive?

Chromate conversion coating, widely known by the trade name Alodine, is a conversion coating: the aluminium surface reacts with a chemical solution and forms a very thin film, with no electric current and no other metal deposited. The film is so thin that part dimensions are effectively unchanged and the surface stays electrically conductive.

That is what makes chromate the right call for electronic parts and enclosures that must be grounded or provide EMI shielding, where an insulating anodized layer gets in the way. Chromate is also a good paint base: applied before powder coating on aluminium, it improves adhesion.

Know its limits, though. The film is soft and thin and does not resist wear and scratching the way anodizing does. Chromate is typically specified per MIL-DTL-5541, which has separate classes for maximum corrosion protection and for low electrical resistance. If your product has to comply with restrictions on hexavalent chromium, say so when you order.

Why plate brass with silver or gold?

Brass is a common choice for electrical parts and fittings because it machines easily and conducts reasonably well. But brass oxidises and dulls in air, and its contact resistance creeps up over time. HyperFab carries out silver and gold plating on brass and other metals for production runs, and it pairs naturally with batch CNC turning of brass parts: the turned part comes back plated and ready to fit.

Which one to choose depends on use, environment and budget. Silver costs less but tarnishes; gold costs more but does not oxidise. Gold on brass is usually applied over an underlayer such as nickel, which stops the base metal from migrating into the gold. The three main reasons to plate brass:

  • Conductivity: silver has the highest electrical conductivity of any metal, so it is used on busbars, high-current contacts and RF parts.
  • Reliable contact: gold does not oxidise, so its contact resistance stays stable over time; the choice for signal connectors and electronics.
  • Appearance: a bright silver or gold look for hardware, statuettes, badges and corporate gifts.

Which finish suits my part? Choosing by base metal and use

Fix the base metal first, then the part's main requirement. If you are still unsure, describe the part's use and environment over the phone:

  • Aluminium part that needs wear and corrosion resistance, in black or a colour: anodizing.
  • Aluminium part that must stay conductive, be grounded or be painted later: chromate (Alodine).
  • Aluminium part that needs both: anodizing with the contact areas masked, or chromate all over, depending on whether wear or conductivity matters more.
  • Brass or copper contacts, busbars or connectors carrying high current: silver plating.
  • Signal connectors, or contacts that must stay reliable in humid conditions: gold plating.
  • Steel parts: aluminium anodizing and chromate do not apply; powder coating is the usual choice for protection and appearance.

How to prepare drawings for plating and anodizing (masking)

Most problems in batch finishing start with an ambiguous drawing. Masking means temporarily covering part of the component with plugs, tape or a special lacquer so the finish does not reach it. It is manual work and adds cost, so mask only what really needs it. When parts are finished on racks, the contact point with the rack is usually left bare; mark a hidden surface for it. Parts should also arrive free of burrs, oil and swarf, because a finish does not hide surface defects and often makes them more visible.

Before you send the drawing, make sure it or the order notes state:

  • The exact material and alloy, for example the brass grade or aluminium alloy.
  • The finish, colour and gloss, and any standard it is specified to (for example ISO 7599 for anodizing).
  • Surfaces that must stay uncoated: threads, precision bores, mating faces and grounding points.
  • Whether the drawing dimensions apply before or after finishing, especially for threads and fits.

What affects the cost of plating, anodizing and chromate?

Each project is quoted after the part has been reviewed. The main factors are:

  • Finish type: gold plating costs more than silver, anodizing or chromate because of the precious metal.
  • The coating thickness called out on the drawing and any standard requirements.
  • Part size, surface area and shape, and how many parts fit on a rack or in a tank at once.
  • Masking, and how many surfaces must stay uncoated.
  • Anodizing colour and any requirement for colour matching between batches.
  • The condition of the parts and the preparation needed before finishing.
  • Quantity: the service is for production runs, and one-off parts are accepted only rarely.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Can steel be anodized?
No. In industry, anodizing is mainly used on aluminium and a few other light metals; steel does not form a protective, adherent oxide, and its oxide is simply rust. HyperFab anodizes aluminium only, and powder coating is the usual finish for steel parts.
Is chromate conversion conductive?
Yes. The chromate (Alodine) film is thin enough that the aluminium surface stays electrically conductive. That is why it is chosen for enclosures and parts that must be grounded, whereas anodizing insulates.
Does anodizing change part dimensions?
Yes, slightly. Part of the anodic layer penetrates into the metal and part builds up on the surface, so outside dimensions grow a little and holes shrink a little. For threads and precision fits, either mask those areas or state on the drawing that dimensions apply after finishing.
Why plate brass with silver?
Bare brass oxidises in air and its contact resistance rises. Silver plating gives it the highest conductivity of any metal and a bright white finish, which suits contacts, busbars and connectors. Silver tarnishes over time, so where stable contact over the long term is critical, gold is the better choice.
