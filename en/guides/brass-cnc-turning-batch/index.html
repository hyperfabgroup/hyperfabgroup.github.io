Batch CNC Turning of Brass Fittings: What Drives the Price

Updated: 15 November 2026

The price of batch-turned brass fittings depends on the brass alloy and bar size, the part's complexity (threads, holes and secondary operations), the tolerances on fit and sealing surfaces, the quantity, and any finish such as plating. Machine setup is spread across the whole run, which is why HyperFab takes this work as production runs.

Where does the cost of a batch-turned brass fitting come from?

Every turned brass fitting is priced from a handful of components: the brass itself, machine time per part, setup (programming, tooling, workholding and first-article approval), secondary operations such as threading and knurling, dimensional inspection, and any final finish. How much each one weighs depends on the design and the quantity.

At low quantities, setup is a large share of each part's price. As the run grows, that share shrinks and machine time and material take over. That is why, in batch work, designing the part to be easier to make, for example dropping operations that add nothing or choosing a sensible bar diameter, is where most of the savings are.

Why are fittings made from brass? Brass vs steel vs stainless

Brass, an alloy of copper and zinc, is the standard material for pipe fittings, valves, nozzles, bushings and mating parts. There are three main reasons: short chips and fast, clean cutting; good corrosion resistance in water and ordinary atmospheres; and a surface that takes silver and gold plating well. It also conducts electricity well, so it shows up in connectors and electrical parts.

Carbon steel, by contrast, is stronger and cheaper as raw material but rusts without a coating, while stainless steel resists corrosion very well but cuts harder and slower. The table compares the three qualitatively for turned fittings.

Why is brass fitting turning done in batches only?

A lot happens before the first acceptable part comes off the machine: drawing review, programming, choosing and setting tools, workholding, making the first article, then measuring and adjusting until it is approved. That cost is roughly the same whether you need one part or a thousand.

In a production run, that cost is spread over the whole batch and the price per part stays reasonable. That is why HyperFab's batch CNC turning service is for production runs: one-off parts are accepted only rarely, and a suitable quantity is recommended after reviewing the project. For single parts and prototypes, CNC machining on hyperfab.ir is the better fit.

In batch work, the real challenge is not making one good part. It is making hundreds or thousands of fittings that are all exactly as good as the approved sample. You approve the first article before the run starts, and dimensions are checked during production and again before delivery.

Cheap turner or expensive turner: what are you actually paying for?

Two quotes for the same drawing can be far apart. Part of the gap is work that the cheaper quote simply leaves out: nobody checks before cutting which face seals, which diameter has to mate with another part, or which thread needs gauging. The result tends to show up at assembly as a leak, a thread that will not tighten properly, or a part that will not seat. With brass fittings, a bad batch usually costs more than the original price difference, because the defect often appears only after plating and assembly.

Precision beyond what the part needs is a cost too. HyperFab matches the production method and precision level to each part's requirements: no more expensive than needed, never below requirements. In a sound quote, this is what you are buying:

  • Drawing engineering before production: identifying fit surfaces, critical diameters, concentricity and sealing faces.
  • A technical briefing for the machinist, so they know where each part needs which accuracy and how it will be measured.
  • First-article approval before the production run starts.
  • Dimensional inspection during production and before delivery.
  • Suitable packing, so threads and fit surfaces arrive undamaged.

How do threads, knurling and secondary operations affect price?

Few brass fittings are a plain turned profile. Internal and external threads, knurling for hand grip, cross holes, wrench flats or hexes, O-ring grooves and deburring each add time, and some add a separate operation.

The earlier and more completely these are defined on the drawing, the more accurate the quote, and the less chance of unplanned costs later.

  • Threads: state the thread type and standard (tapered or parallel pipe thread, metric or inch) and its class precisely; gauged threads take longer to inspect.
  • Knurling: specify the pattern (straight or diamond) and where it runs on the part.
  • Hexes and flats: starting from hexagonal bar avoids a separate milling step and brings cost down.
  • Cross holes and milling: anything the lathe cannot do in the same setup may need re-clamping and more time.
  • Deburring and cleaning: on water and gas fittings and on parts going for plating, leftover burrs and oil cause trouble.

Can brass parts be plated after turning?

Yes. Brass is a good base for silver and gold plating, and you can hand the plating to HyperFab in the same project and receive the part turned, plated and ready to use. Silver plating gives the highest electrical conductivity and is common on contacts and connectors; gold does not oxidise and keeps a stable contact resistance.

If a part will be plated, plan for it from the start. Plating builds a layer on the surface, so precision threads and fit surfaces have to be designed and turned with that in mind. Mark any surfaces that must stay unplated on the drawing. Turned finish and deburring also show directly in the final appearance, because plating does not hide surface defects.

Plating, like turning, is a batch service. If the part needs a decorative or electrical finish, or no finish at all, say so in the enquiry so it is reflected in the quote.

What to send for a quote, and what the price depends on

A dimensioned drawing with tolerances is the key document. Send a 3D model (a STEP file, for example) if you have one, but it does not replace the drawing. The final price depends on:

  • The brass alloy and the bar size and shape (round or hexagonal)
  • Part complexity: the number of steps, threads, holes and secondary operations
  • Tolerances, fit surfaces and sealing faces
  • Quantity and whether the order repeats
  • Finish: silver plating, gold plating or none
  • Special inspection, such as thread gauging or a dimensional report
  • Packing requirements

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Do you accept single pieces?
HyperFab's batch CNC turning service is for production runs; one-off parts are accepted only rarely, and a suitable quantity is recommended after reviewing the project. For single parts and prototypes, see CNC machining on hyperfab.ir.
Can parts be plated after turning?
Yes. Silver and gold plating on brass parts is available at HyperFab, and the finished part is delivered. If plating is needed, mention it up front so threads and fit surfaces are reviewed with the coating in mind.
What file do you need for a quote?
A dimensioned drawing with tolerances, thread specifications, the material and the quantity. A 3D model such as a STEP file helps but does not replace the drawing. If you have no drawing, a sample part or a phone description can be a starting point.
Which brass alloy should I use for fittings?
Turned fittings are usually made from free-cutting (leaded) brass, which breaks into short chips and machines quickly. Parts in contact with drinking water may need a low-lead or lead-free brass under the product standard. The designer or the product standard sets the alloy, and it can be discussed during the drawing review.
